# Method

## Data Collection
To compare the use of grammatical gender in Standard German and German Easy Language, a parallel corpus consisting of the same texts written in both variants is necessary. \textit{ELGEPA} \cite{easy-language-translation} is utilized for this purpose, as it contains Easy Language--Standard German text pairs sourced from municipal websites across Germany (Dresden, Hannover, Hessen, München, Hamburg).

## Data Preparation
Because the utilized ELGEPA corpus is internally pre-processed, deduplicated, and formatted as an aligned parallel dataset, complex semantic mapping was omitted to preserve the corpus's original breadth. The data preparation for this analysis is therefore strictly limited to alignment verification to ensure basic structural integrity prior to NLP processing. To achieve this without discarding legitimate document summaries, three hard logical filters are enforced. First, any text pairs originating from purely administrative web pages (e.g., imprints, privacy policies, or contact forms) are preemptively excluded via URL keyword matching. Second, a minimum length sanity filter excludes any text pairs containing fewer than 20 tokens on either side, preventing extremely short navigational artifacts from introducing noise. Third, a robust length-ratio outlier detection is performed using the Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) of the logarithmic token-length ratio per source. Pairs exhibiting extreme structural asymmetry (modified Z-score $\ge 3.5$) are rejected. This guarantees that only structurally plausible parallel pairs are passed to the subsequent NLP-based actor extraction pipeline.

## Gender Bias Analysis in Standard German and Easy Language Documents
To analyze textual gender bias, two complementary approaches are applied to the accepted parallel corpus.

### Actor Extraction
Each document is processed with a German spaCy model to identify human actors. Named entity recognition (NER) is used to extract person entities, and a curated lexicon of generic person nouns (e.g., \textit{Arzt}, \textit{Politikerin}, \textit{Studierende}) is matched against token lemmas. Optionally, a fine-tuned BERT model for German personal noun detection \cite{10936848} and a GLiNER-based role extractor can supplement the spaCy pipeline. For each identified actor, the senteces in which it appears are extracted as predications. Gender is inferred through two mechanisms: (1) grammatical pronoun linking (masculine/feminine pronoun co-occurrence), and (2) a mDeBERTa-based zero-shot classifier \cite{laurer_less_2023} that predicts the gender context of each actor from its predication sentences. This yields an actor-level dataset with one row per actor mention cluster, including the actor label, nomination count, predicted gender, and associated sentences.


## Image Generation from Standard German and Easy Language Prompts
To investigate whether the linguistic gender framing in the two text variants propagates into visual representations, images are generated from actor-level prompts derived from the parallel corpus. For each actor, two prompts are constructed: a \textit{lexical baseline prompt} consisting solely of the actor noun (e.g., \textit{Ein Arzt.}), and a \textit{minimal context prompt} that additionally includes the first predication sentence of the actor (e.g., \textit{Ein Arzt. Der Arzt erklärt die Diagnose.}). Only topic pairs for which actors were identified on \textit{both} language sides are retained, enabling a matched-pair comparison. Images are generated using FLUX.2[klein] \cite{bfl2025representation}, a state-of-the-art text-to-image model with native multilingual support. For each actor prompt, five images are generated using fixed random seeds shared across both language sides, ensuring reproducibility and enabling paired statistical comparisons.

## Analysis of Effects of Prompts on Generated Images
To quantify the gender bias reflected in the generated images, the MiVOLO model \cite{kuprashevich2023mivolo} is applied to all generated images. MiVOLO jointly predicts the age and gender of detected persons using both face and body cues. For each image, the number of detected persons, their individual gender predictions, and the dominant gender are recorded. 

Images in which MiVOLO detects zero persons are excluded from gender evaluation, but recorded to compare the overall "no-person rate" between the language variants. The remaining valid images are mapped into five categorical representation labels matching the text analysis: \textit{weiblich (Singular)} (exactly one female), \textit{maskulin (Singular)} (exactly one male), \textit{Frauengruppe} (multiple persons, all female), \textit{Männergruppe} (multiple persons, all male), and \textit{gemischte/generische Gruppe} (multiple persons, mixed genders). 

This categorical alignment allows the image data to be analyzed via a comparative matrix. To identify the most prominent depicted person in each image, the face bounding box with the largest pixel area is selected as a proxy for visual salience, yielding a per-image \textit{dominant gender} label. Face bounding boxes are used rather than person body bounding boxes for this purpose, as all MiVOLO-classifiable detections are associated with a face crop regardless of whether a full body was detected, making face box areas directly comparable across detections; person body boxes, by contrast, vary widely in aspect ratio and scale and cannot meaningfully be compared to face-only detections.

To assess whether the categorical distribution of generated visual representations differs significantly between Standard German and German Easy Language, a Chi-Square Test of Independence ($\chi^2$) is applied. However, a naive overall test conflates two sources of variation: genuine differences in how each language variant is interpreted by the text-to-image model, and differences in the composition of textual gender categories present in the two sides of the corpus (e.g., the proportion of prompts labeled \textit{generisch maskulin (Singular)} is 76.4\,\% for German Easy Language versus 73.8\,\% for Standard German). To disentangle these effects, a \textit{stratified} $\chi^2$ test is conducted as the primary statistical analysis: the test is applied separately within each textually predicted gender category, comparing the distribution of visual output categories between the two language sides while holding the type of actor prompt constant. This design directly addresses the research question of whether the language variant itself -- rather than the underlying actor composition -- drives differences in the generated imagery. The naive overall test is additionally reported as a descriptive summary. Furthermore, the visual categories are directly cross-referenced with the textually predicted gender categories from the actor extraction. This enables the evaluation of semantic alignment accuracy, revealing whether the text-to-image generator accurately reflects the text's intended gender composition (e.g., rendering a generic text group as visually mixed) or whether it defaults to a stereotypical visual collapse (e.g., rendering a generic text group as a \textit{Männergruppe}) across the two language variants.

Beyond the category-level analysis, a supplementary investigation is conducted on images classified as \textit{gemischte/generische Gruppe}, i.e., images in which both male and female persons were detected. Even within this category, a systematic gender skew may persist: the dominant person -- identified as the individual with the largest face bounding box -- may be disproportionately male in one language variant. The dominant gender is compared between Standard German and German Easy Language using a Chi-Square test on the 2$\times$2 contingency table of dominant gender by language side. Additionally, the distribution of the male-count ratio (share of male detections among all detected persons in the image) is compared using a two-sided Mann-Whitney $U$ test, which does not assume normality and is appropriate for ratio-valued data. This analysis captures a second-order masculine bias that would remain invisible in the categorical analysis alone: even when both language variants generate mixed-gender imagery, one variant may consistently foreground male subjects.
