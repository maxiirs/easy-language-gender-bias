# Results

## Image Gender Bias in Generated Images

### No-Person Rate and Category Distribution
Of the 7{,}995 generated images analyzed (3{,}685 from German Easy Language prompts, 4{,}310 from Standard German prompts), MiVOLO failed to detect any person in 310 Easy Language images (8.4\,\%) and 358 Standard German images (8.3\,\%). The near-identical no-person rates indicate that the two language variants are equally capable of eliciting person-containing imagery from the text-to-image model, and that differences in the gender distribution of the remaining images are not attributable to differential detection failure. Excluding images with no detected persons, the distribution of visual gender categories differs substantially between the two sides. German Easy Language prompts produce \textit{maskulin (Singular)} images at a rate of 40.7\,\%, compared to 31.9\,\% for Standard German. Conversely, Standard German prompts yield a higher share of \textit{weiblich (Singular)} images (29.4\,\% vs.\ 25.5\,\%) and \textit{gemischte/generische Gruppe} images (19.8\,\% vs.\ 14.9\,\%).

### Statistical Tests on Category-Level Distributions
An overall Chi-Square Test of Independence confirms that the two distributions differ significantly ($\chi^2 = 81.34$, $df = 5$, $p < 0.001$). However, as noted in the method section, the two corpus sides differ slightly in their composition of textually predicted gender categories (76.4\,\% vs.\ 73.8\,\% \textit{generisch maskulin (Singular)}), which constitutes a potential confound. The stratified $\chi^2$ test, conducted separately within each textual gender category, confirms that the language variant effect persists after controlling for prompt composition. For the \textit{generisch maskulin (Singular)} stratum -- the dominant category in both corpora -- the difference is highly significant ($\chi^2 = 76.07$, $df = 5$, $p < 0.001$), as is the \textit{maskulin (Singular)} stratum ($\chi^2 = 40.79$, $df = 5$, $p < 0.001$). The \textit{gemischte/generische Gruppe} stratum yields a significant result at the 5\,\% level ($\chi^2 = 10.76$, $df = 3$, $p = 0.013$), though the small sample sizes (15 and 25 images) warrant caution. The \textit{weiblich (Singular)} stratum does not reach significance ($\chi^2 = 9.18$, $df = 5$, $p = 0.102$), indicating that the language variant has no statistically reliable effect on the visual output for explicitly feminine prompts. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the language-variant effect on generated gender is concentrated in prompts with masculine or generic masculine framing, and cannot be attributed to differences in corpus composition.

### Visual Collapse of the Generic Masculine
The most substantive finding concerns the visual rendering of \textit{generisch maskulin (Singular)} actors, i.e., role nouns whose grammatical gender is masculine but whose referent is intended generically. For these prompts, the text-to-image model generates a \textit{maskulin (Singular)} image -- depicting a single man -- in 41.3\,\% of Easy Language cases versus 31.2\,\% of Standard German cases, a difference of more than ten percentage points. Standard German prompts, by contrast, more frequently yield \textit{gemischte/generische Gruppe} images (20.5\,\% vs.\ 16.3\,\%) and \textit{weiblich (Singular)} images (28.6\,\% vs.\ 23.9\,\%). This pattern suggests that the phrasing characteristic of German Easy Language -- typically shorter, more syntactically reduced, and reliant on the bare generic masculine noun -- transmits a stronger masculine gender signal to the text-to-image model than the corresponding Standard German formulations, which embed the same noun in richer sentential context. The result is a systematic \textit{visual collapse} of gender-neutral language into masculine imagery, and this collapse is demonstrably more pronounced in German Easy Language than in Standard German.

### Semantic Alignment Accuracy
The semantic alignment accuracy -- the share of images whose visual gender category matches the textually predicted category -- reflects the inherent difficulty of the \textit{generisch maskulin (Singular)} class: since no direct visual equivalent for a grammatically masculine but referentially gender-neutral noun exists in the five-category scheme, alignment is zero for this class by construction. For explicitly gendered actors, alignment is substantially higher and advantages German Easy Language slightly for female actors (\textit{weiblich (Singular)}: 72.1\,\% vs.\ 64.6\,\%) and male actors (\textit{maskulin (Singular)}: 59.4\,\% vs.\ 52.8\,\%). Overall alignment rates are low for both variants (14.8\,\% for Easy Language, 15.1\,\% for Standard German), primarily because the \textit{generisch maskulin (Singular)} category, for which alignment is zero, constitutes the majority of prompts.

### Dominant-Gender Analysis
Restricting to images with at least one detected person, German Easy Language prompts yield a male dominant figure (largest face bounding box) in 62.0\,\% of cases, compared to 53.1\,\% for Standard German. This nine-percentage-point gap indicates that beyond the distribution of visual gender categories, Easy Language prompts more consistently position male subjects as the visually foregrounded individual even in images that would be classified as depicting a group.

### Gender Skew Within Mixed-Group Images
A supplementary analysis restricted to the 1{,}403 images classified as \textit{gemischte/generische Gruppe} reveals a further layer of masculine bias in German Easy Language. Within this subset, the dominant person is male in 60.5\,\% of Easy Language images but in only 49.3\,\% of Standard German images -- a difference that is highly significant ($\chi^2 = 13.61$, $df = 1$, $p < 0.001$). Notably, the average male-count ratio within the group is slightly \textit{higher} in Standard German (57.2\,\%) than in Easy Language (55.6\,\%), which rules out the possibility that Easy Language simply generates more male persons per image. The Mann-Whitney $U$ test on the male-count ratio distributions corroborates this with a marginally significant result ($U = 220{,}125.5$, $p = 0.047$). Taken together, these findings indicate that the masculine foreground bias in Easy Language operates at the level of \textit{visual salience} -- male subjects are placed closer to the camera and occupy more of the frame -- rather than through a greater absolute number of male detections per image. This constitutes a second-order masculine bias that is invisible to category-level analysis alone.
